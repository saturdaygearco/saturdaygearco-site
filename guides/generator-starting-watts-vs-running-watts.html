Generator Starting Watts vs. Running Watts

The biggest wattage on the box is not the amount a generator can supply continuously.

By Nicholas · Published October 1, 2026

Quick answer: Running watts are the power a generator can supply continuously. Starting watts are the higher, short-duration output available when a motor or compressor starts. Add the running watts of everything that will operate together, then add the additional startup demand of the largest motor likely to start while those loads are running. Compare the first number with the generator's running rating and the second with its starting rating.

What each wattage number means

Generator running wattsContinuous power the generator is rated to deliver under the maker's stated conditions.Your simultaneous continuous load must fit here, with practical headroom.
Generator starting or peak wattsShort-duration maximum intended to cover startup demand.Your running load plus the relevant temporary surge must fit here. It is not the continuous rating.
Appliance running wattsPower used while the equipment operates normally.Add the equipment expected to run at the same time.
Appliance starting wattsThe brief demand when a motor, pump or compressor starts.Determine whether the source states total startup demand or watts additional to running load before doing the arithmetic.

The sizing formula

Required running capacity = total simultaneous running watts

Required starting capacity = total running watts + largest relevant additional startup demand

Westinghouse uses this same basic method in the WGen9500DF manual: total the running load, identify the device with the highest starting demand, and make sure the generator covers the combined requirement. The important detail is whether your appliance information lists a total starting value or an additional surge value. Do not add the full starting number on top of running watts if that starting number already includes the running portion.

Worked example: one compressor load

These figures are arithmetic examples, not specifications for your refrigerator:

  • Refrigerator: 700 running watts
  • Lights: 100 running watts
  • Router: 100 running watts
  • Refrigerator: 1,200 additional starting watts

The continuous load is 900 watts. When the refrigerator starts, the temporary demand is 2,100 watts: 900 running watts plus 1,200 additional starting watts. The generator therefore needs a running rating above the continuous load and a starting rating above the temporary demand, plus sensible operating margin.

Why you usually do not add every surge

Motors do not necessarily start at the same instant. If a refrigerator and a well pump can be managed so they start separately, sizing around the largest relevant surge may be reasonable. If automatic equipment can overlap or reliable power is critical, the plan must account for that overlap. Staggering loads can reduce the capacity required, but only when someone can actually control the sequence.

Three common mistakes

  1. Shopping by peak watts: a 12,500-watt starting label does not mean 12,500 watts are available continuously.
  2. Using a generic appliance chart as an exact measurement: model, age, operating mode and motor design can change demand. Use the equipment label, manual or manufacturer information.
  3. Sizing from gasoline output while using propane: dual-fuel models can have lower running and starting ratings on propane. Use the rating for the fuel you will actually run.

Leave room for real operation

A calculation that lands exactly on the generator's rating leaves little room for changing loads, cycling compressors or measurement uncertainty. Manufacturer instructions control for the exact unit. Also consider fuel use, noise, extension-cord limits, outlet configuration and whether the equipment needs 120 or 240 volts; enough total watts do not fix an incompatible connection.

Safety: Fuel-powered generators belong outdoors, far from doors, windows and vents, with exhaust directed away from occupied spaces. Never operate one inside a home, garage or other enclosed area, even with doors open. Use working carbon-monoxide alarms and follow the manufacturer's placement instructions. Never backfeed a household outlet; use properly installed transfer equipment when powering home circuits.

Bottom line

Running watts determine whether the generator can carry the normal load. Starting watts determine whether it can survive the brief surge without overload. Build the continuous load first, add the largest relevant additional startup demand, use the fuel-specific ratings and leave practical room instead of purchasing by the largest number printed on the generator.

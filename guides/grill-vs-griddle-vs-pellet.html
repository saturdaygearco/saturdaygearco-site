Gas Grill vs. Griddle vs. Pellet Grill

Three excellent ways to cook outside. The right one depends less on the spec sheet and more on what you actually want to cook on Saturday.

Quick answer: Buy a gas grill for fast, familiar grilling and easy weeknight use. Pick a griddle for smash burgers, breakfast, fajitas and high-volume flat-top cooking. Choose a pellet grill when barbecue, smoke flavor and hands-off low-and-slow temperature control matter most.

Gas grill: the fast all-rounder

A gas grill is still the easiest recommendation for someone who wants traditional backyard grilling without much ceremony. Turn the burners on, preheat, and you can be cooking burgers, chicken, vegetables, hot dogs or steaks in minutes. Multiple burner zones also make it easy to keep one side hot for searing and another cooler for finishing food indirectly.

The open grates give you flame-driven browning and let fat drip away, but small or delicate foods can be awkward without a basket or griddle insert. Gas also produces less smoke character than burning wood or pellets. If your ideal cookout is mostly burgers, steaks, chicken and vegetables—and you value speed—a gas grill is difficult to beat.

Griddle: maximum contact and cooking space

A griddle trades open grates for a solid steel surface. That changes what the cooker does well. Smash burgers get full-surface crust, chopped vegetables stay on the cooktop, and eggs, pancakes, bacon, cheesesteaks and fried rice become realistic outdoor options. A large flat top can also feed a crowd efficiently because nearly the entire surface is usable.

The tradeoff is maintenance. You need to scrape the cooktop, manage grease and keep a carbon-steel surface seasoned and protected from moisture. A griddle also does not reproduce the same open-flame grilling experience, and it is not the right tool for traditional smoking. If your menu looks more like a diner, taco stand or burger joint than a barbecue pit, though, it may be the most useful cooker you can own.

Pellet grill: barbecue with easier temperature control

Pellet grills use an electric controller and auger to feed compressed wood pellets into a fire pot. Their biggest advantage is maintaining a selected cooking temperature with far less fire management than a traditional charcoal or offset smoker. That makes them particularly attractive for ribs, pork shoulder, brisket, chicken, roasting and other longer cooks.

The compromises are worth understanding. They require electricity, have more mechanical and electronic components than a basic gas grill, and consume pellets that must stay dry. High-temperature performance also varies significantly by design. Some models offer dedicated searing features, but shoppers should not assume every pellet grill will sear like a hot gas grill or flat top.

Which cooker fits what you make?

Burgers and steaks

Gas is the conventional choice for grill marks, direct heat and quick temperature changes. A griddle is arguably better if your definition of the perfect burger is a thin smash burger with an edge-to-edge crust. Pellet grills can make excellent meat, but searing capability deserves extra scrutiny if steaks are your priority.

Breakfast and tailgate food

The griddle wins. Eggs, bacon, pancakes, hash browns, quesadillas and chopped ingredients all benefit from a continuous surface. It is also easy to run multiple food zones when feeding a group.

Ribs, brisket and pulled pork

Pellet is the clear fit among these three. Stable temperatures and wood smoke are exactly what long barbecue cooks need. Gas can cook indirectly, but adding smoke and maintaining the desired environment generally takes more improvisation.

Quick weeknight dinners

Gas and griddle both excel because they preheat quickly and respond immediately when you change burner output. Which one wins depends on whether you prefer open grates or a flat cooking surface.

Don't ignore fuel and ownership

The purchase price is only part of the decision. Gas grills and most outdoor griddles typically run on propane or natural gas, while pellet grills need both wood pellets and an electrical outlet. Your local fuel prices, cooking frequency and typical cook duration determine the real operating cost, so broad claims that one fuel is always cheaper are not especially useful.

Storage matters too. Pellets need to remain dry. Steel griddle tops need protection from moisture and corrosion. Gas systems need periodic hose, regulator and burner inspection. Any cooker lasts longer when it is covered, cleaned and kept out of standing water.

What size should you buy?

Ignore the urge to buy the largest cooker that fits the patio. Think about the number of people you normally feed, then leave some extra capacity for guests. A couple cooking for themselves usually benefits more from quick preheat and manageable cleanup than enormous surface area. Families and frequent hosts should prioritize usable primary cooking space rather than inflated totals that include warming racks.

For a griddle, surface layout matters because every ingredient competes for flat-top space. For a pellet grill, make sure the main grate can physically hold the cuts you intend to smoke. For gas, consider burner count and whether the layout lets you create distinct direct and indirect heat zones.

Can one cooker do everything?

Not perfectly. Accessories can blur the lines—a gas grill can accept a griddle plate, some pellet grills add direct-flame zones, and griddles can cook an enormous range of foods—but each platform still has a core strength. Buying for that strength usually produces a better experience than buying around a long accessory list.

Best two-cooker combinations: Gas + griddle covers fast traditional grilling and flat-top cooking. Pellet + griddle covers smoking plus high-heat flat-top meals. If barbecue is only an occasional interest, start with the cooker you will actually fire up every week rather than building a three-cooker patio immediately.

What to compare before buying

Once you know which category fits, compare the details that affect ownership: usable cooking area, burner or temperature-zone control, grease management, weather protection, replacement-part availability, warranty, cart stability and how easily the cooking surfaces can be cleaned. For pellet grills, also compare hopper capacity and low-temperature control. For griddles, look closely at grease-drain placement and lid or hood availability. For gas grills, burner quality and replacement availability matter more than flashy side features.

Bottom line

For most people who want conventional backyard cooking, gas is the safest all-around starting point. If you constantly make breakfast, smash burgers, fajitas or food for a crowd, a griddle may get used more often. If your weekends revolve around ribs, pork shoulder, brisket and smoke flavor, pellet is the purpose-built choice.

The best cooker is not the one with the longest feature list. It is the one whose cooking surface and workflow match the food you make often enough to justify the patio space.

Cooking equipment involves hot surfaces, flame and fuel. Follow the manufacturer's clearance, ventilation, fuel-storage and operating instructions for your specific appliance.

What Size Air Compressor Do You Need?

Start with the tool—not the tank. CFM and PSI determine whether a compressor can actually keep up.

Quick answer: Find the highest-CFM air tool you expect to run, confirm its required PSI, then choose a compressor that can deliver more CFM at that pressure. Tank size mainly affects how long you can work before the pump cycles.

CFM matters most

CFM measures air volume. Nailers use air in short bursts, while grinders, sanders and other continuous-use tools can demand much more sustained airflow. Compare the tool's CFM requirement with the compressor's rated CFM at the required PSI—not just the horsepower number on the box.

PSI is pressure, not capacity

A compressor may advertise a high maximum PSI but still lack enough airflow for a demanding tool. Both numbers matter: the compressor must reach the required pressure and maintain adequate CFM there.

How much CFM headroom should you buy?

Do not size a compressor so its published output exactly matches your most demanding tool. Ratings, hose losses and real working conditions can leave an exact-match setup running near its limit. Practical headroom gives the pump more room to recover and makes future tool additions less painful. If two tools may run at the same time, account for their combined demand rather than sizing for only one.

What tank size changes

Small tanks are portable and work well for inflation and intermittent fastening. Larger tanks provide a bigger air reserve and reduce how frequently the motor must recover during sustained work. A larger tank cannot compensate for a pump that produces too little CFM.

Intermittent vs. continuous-use tools

For brad nailers, finish nailers and occasional tire inflation, portability can matter more than a huge tank. Die grinders, sanders and other tools that consume air continuously place much more emphasis on pump output and duty cycle. Impact wrenches sit between those extremes because actual air use depends heavily on how long and how often the trigger is held.

Garage buying checklist

List the tools you actually plan to use. Check their CFM at operating PSI. Add reasonable airflow headroom, then decide how much portability and tank capacity you want. Also consider noise, electrical requirements, duty cycle, drain access and whether replacement parts are readily available.

Before you buy

Check the electrical circuit the compressor requires, especially for larger stationary units. Think about where the machine will sit, how you will drain condensation, hose length, noise near living spaces and whether you need to move the compressor around the property. Those ownership details often matter more than a small difference in advertised maximum PSI.

Bottom line

For most garage buyers, sizing by the most demanding tool is more reliable than shopping by tank gallons or horsepower alone. Buy enough airflow for the work you expect to do, with some margin so the compressor is not constantly operating at its limit.

Pool Day Simple Scoop: What Changed in My Pool Routine

My experience with a simpler weekly dosing task—and the pool care it does not replace.

By Nicholas, founder and editor • Published September 27, 2026

Short answer: I use Pool Day Simple Scoop in my roughly 20,000-gallon in-ground gunite pool. It has made my weekly chemical addition much simpler. I still use chlorine tablets in the pool's feeder: Simple Scoop is not a sanitizer, and a quick dosing task is not a substitute for checking pool water.

What I can say from ownership

Before Simple Scoop, my chemical routine could take hours as I worked through separate products and decisions. Adding the weekly product now takes me about two minutes. That is my experience with the application step, not a claim that all pool maintenance takes two minutes or that everyone will get the same result. My pool is approximately 20,000 gallons, in-ground and gunite; I use chlorine tablets in the feeder alongside Simple Scoop.

I have not run a controlled comparison of water quality, annual chemical spending or labor hours. I cannot promise that the product will solve algae, replace balancing chemicals in every pool or lower your costs. Pool conditions, sanitizer systems and local water vary.

What Simple Scoop does—and does not—replace

Pool Day describes Simple Scoop as an all-in-one weekly care product used besides sanitizer. Its product page says it is added directly to the water without pre-mixing. That distinction matters: buying it does not remove the need for a suitable chlorine or other compatible sanitizer system. Follow the current package directions, check compatibility and do not mix pool chemicals together outside those directions.

The phrase “two-minute pool care” describes the weekly addition, not the full responsibility of owning a safe pool. Filtration, cleaning and water testing remain separate jobs. A product that makes dosing easier cannot tell you today's free-chlorine or pH reading.

Water testing still matters

The CDC's residential-pool guidance recommends testing chlorine and pH at least twice a day, and more often with heavy use. It recommends pH of 7.0–7.8 and at least 1 ppm chlorine in a pool, or at least 2 ppm when cyanuric acid is used. The CDC says a DPD kit gives more accurate results than test strips. Those are public-health recommendations, independent of my product experience or Pool Day's marketing. If readings are out of range, address the water before swimming according to the test-kit and chemical labels.

Should you buy it?

Simple Scoop is worth investigating if your current routine involves juggling several separate products and you value a simpler weekly addition. First, check Pool Day's compatibility questions for your sanitizer and current treatment approach. Then compare the cost for your actual pool volume and season against what you currently buy. Confirm the dose and package size on the current label; do not extrapolate my pool's routine to yours.

It is a poor shortcut if you are hoping to stop sanitizing or testing, or if you have a persistent water problem whose cause has not been identified. Test and correct the water issue rather than assuming another scoop will fix it.

My buying checklist:
  • Is my sanitizer system compatible?
  • What dose does the current label require for my pool volume?
  • What would a season of use cost compared with my present routine?
  • Do I have a reliable way to check chlorine and pH independently?
